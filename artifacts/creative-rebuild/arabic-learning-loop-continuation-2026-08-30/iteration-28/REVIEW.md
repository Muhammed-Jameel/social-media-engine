## Why it passes

- `سمِّ الأشياء كما يسمّيها الناس` is concise, actionable Arabic rather than an abstract plea for “user centricity.”
- The pale diagonal lane behaves like public language entering the brand field; it performs the idea without a speech bubble, quote, UI card, or workshop artifact.
- `يقولونها / يبحثون بها / يفهمونها` turns the evidence chain into a readable three-beat rhythm instead of another process diagram.
- The first render's cropped `كما` was rejected. The final live phrase is complete at both sizes.
- At 324×405, the principal headline is approximately 31.8 px, diagonal phrase 32.4 px, language trail 12.6 px, support 13.2 px, and reference 10.2 px.

## Evidence audit

- GOV.UK explicitly advises teams to learn the language people use while talking about a service.
- Its clear-language guidance recommends words users know and use themselves and points teams to common search terms.
- W3C's supplemental cognitive-accessibility guidance supports common, clear words and explaining unfamiliar jargon.
- The post does not claim that familiar language alone proves usability, accessibility conformance, or a validated user need.

## Arabic audit

- The colloquial-accessible metadata `لغة الناس` deliberately performs the post's principle while the support retains professional precision.

## Hard-fail audit

- Exact copy, Ghroob regular/bold, canvas bounds, contrast, logo, original/mobile dimensions, hashes, and source legibility: pass.
- No quotation bubble, sticky note, terminology grid, simulated interface, correction mark, photograph, generated image, external visual asset, or professional reference pixel: pass.

## Residual risk

There is no single universal “language of people.” Any production caption or client application must segment by audience and task, use repeated research and search evidence, test labels in context, and explain specialist or legal terms when those terms are necessary.
